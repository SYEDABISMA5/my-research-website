Gender-Based Violence in Pakistan: The Gap Between Legal Protection and Its Implementation

Author: Bisma Gillani

Published: August 2026

Introduction

Pakistan has been an independent state since 1947, and its Constitution provides equal rights and legal protection to all citizens without discrimination on the basis of gender. However, there remains a significant gap between these constitutional guarantees and the reality faced by many women in their daily lives.

Although equality and fundamental rights are recognized in legal documents, their effective implementation remains a serious challenge. Gender-based discrimination and violence continue to affect women across Pakistan, particularly in rural and less-developed communities where women may have limited access to education and may not be fully aware of their basic legal rights.

As a result, many women remain vulnerable to domestic violence, social discrimination, and other forms of abuse within a predominantly male-dominated social structure.

The Rise of Misogynistic Attitudes in Society

Despite living in an increasingly modern and developing society, Pakistan continues to face deeply rooted patriarchal and misogynistic attitudes. In some sections of society, women's empowerment and independence are still viewed as a challenge to traditional male authority.

Narrow-minded attitudes and social dogmatism can restrict women's freedom and discourage them from speaking against discrimination and violence.

Female emancipation should not be considered a threat to society. Rather, women's freedom, dignity, equality, and participation are fundamental components of a democratic and independent state.

Weak Enforcement of Laws

Non-enforcement of laws includes weak governance, inadequate institutional mechanisms, social pressure, and limited accountability. These factors can prevent victims from receiving timely justice.

In many cases, women may hesitate to report violence because of fear of social stigma, family pressure, economic dependence, or lack of confidence in the justice system. This can create an environment in which perpetrators may feel that they can escape accountability.

Khalida Chandio Alleged "Honour" Killing (Sindh)

In July 2026, just 20-year-old Khalida Chandio was reportedly declared kari by a local jirga and allegedly killed by male relatives. The reported incident highlights concerns about the continuing influence of informal tribal practices over Pakistan's formal legal system.

Although jirgas do not have legal authority to issue criminal judgments, social pressure, patriarchal traditions, political influence, and inadequate law enforcement can contribute to weak accountability and hinder victims' access to justice.

Asma Begum: Alleged Murder by Husband (Karachi)

A recent case that highlights the gap between laws and their implementation is the reported case of 58-year-old Asma Begum. In July 2026, her husband reportedly confessed to killing her after she refused to have sexual intercourse.

This reported case reflects the serious problem of domestic and gender-based violence in Pakistan. It also shows that having laws alone is not enough to protect women if these laws are not properly implemented.

The underreporting of domestic violence, social pressure, and difficulties in accessing justice can further weaken the protection available to women. Therefore, the case demonstrates how gaps in law enforcement and accountability can allow gender-based violence to continue despite the existence of legal protections.

Conclusion

The continued prevalence of gender-based violence in Pakistan is not simply a result of a lack of laws. Rather, it reflects the failure to effectively enforce existing laws, weak institutional mechanisms, social and cultural pressures, and the deep-rooted conflict between formal legal protections and customary practices.

Pakistan needs effective implementation of existing laws, stronger institutions, greater accountability, awareness of women's legal rights, and social attitudes that respect women's dignity and equality.

Laws can provide protection on paper, but meaningful change will only occur when those protections are enforced in real life.
